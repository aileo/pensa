# What Pensa does

Pensa keeps track of family gifts so that nobody has to hold it in their head.
This page describes the features; [the API reference](api.md) describes the
routes behind them.

## The rule that shapes everything

**You never see what is meant for you.** Reservations, contributions and
history are hidden from the beneficiary, and the API answers `404` rather than
`403` when they ask — a `403` would itself reveal that something exists. It is
why the app can be shared inside a family without anyone having to be careful
about what they click.

## Wishes

A wish is a title, an optional link, an optional price, tags and a description.
Paste a link and the metadata is fetched automatically: name, image and price
are filled in, and you can correct them before saving. Once a wish exists only
its tags remain editable. Deleting a reserved wish keeps the commitment as a
**private off-list gift**: its details, participants, occasions and progress
survive, while open contributions are disabled.

Someone else's list can be filtered by tag, availability and price range.
Availability is never shown to the owner of the list.

## Households and families

A **household** is the people who live together — they share a list of people
to give to. A **family** connects several households.

Joining is always by invitation code, never by search:

- a **household** invitation code is used during registration, to join an
  existing household;
- a **family** invitation code is generated by a family administrator. An
  administrator **of the invited household** confirms it, or, if the person has
  no account yet, they enter it when registering and get a household of their
  own, already attached to the family. Knowing a household identifier never
  grants access.

Whoever hands out a code does not have to explain which kind it is: the
registration form takes either.

New accounts are **invitation-only by default** on a deployed instance, with one
exception — the very first account of an empty database, so that whoever sets
the instance up can get in. See [deployment](deployment.md#who-can-sign-up).

A household involved in active reservations cannot be removed from a family.

Each family view shows only that family's members — belonging to two families
never mixes their members together. Family administrators can rename the family
and generate invitation codes; household administrators accept them.
They can also appoint and remove family administrators among members with
accounts, without changing anyone's household role. The last family
administrator cannot be removed.

## Members without an account

Children, and anyone else who should not sign in, exist as **managed members**:
real people in the app, with a date of birth, occasions and a wish list, but no
credentials. Any administrator of their household creates them, writes their
list, edits their tags and removes what no longer fits. From the outside nothing
changes — the rest of the family sees an ordinary list and reserves gifts on it
without ever learning who wrote it.

Their record — first name, last name, date of birth and name day — is set when
they are created and can be corrected afterwards, so a missing name day does not
mean creating the person again.

The dashboard reminds the household when a managed list is still empty, so a
child's birthday does not arrive with nothing on their list. It also lists the
managed lists the household keeps, and the same shortcuts sit at the top of *my
wishes*, so switching from your own list to a child's takes one click instead of
a detour through the family screen.

A managed member's list uses the same display and ordering controls as *my
wishes*: drag a wish or use the up/down arrows, and the order persists the next
time anyone opens the list. Reordering only changes priority — it never touches
a wish's details, its reservations or what the rest of the family is allowed to
see. The controls step aside while a filter narrows the list to a subset, since
persisting an order built from only part of the wishes would silently misplace
the hidden ones; clearing the filters brings them back.

Nobody stays managed forever:

- **a claim code** lets the person open their own account from the sign-in
  screen, choosing their own email and password and keeping their wishes;
- **credentials set by an administrator** do the same on the spot, for someone
  who is not there to do it themselves.

Once independent, they can **leave the household**: they get one of their own,
automatically attached to the same families, so the gift circle survives someone
moving out. Either they or an administrator of their household can trigger it, a
household never loses its last member or its last administrator, and a managed
member is never a household or family administrator.

## Occasions

Three kinds:

- **Birthday**, from the date of birth in the profile;
- **Name day**, from the optional `nameDay` field (`MM-DD`). Without it, the
  occasion simply has no next occurrence;
- **Fixed date**, such as Christmas, from a month and a day.

Occasions belong to a family, so someone who belongs to two families has the
same occasion stored twice. The interface deduplicates them, and orders them
chronologically from today rather than alphabetically. Each upcoming occasion on
the dashboard opens the wish list of the person concerned, so an approaching
date leads straight to what they would like.

## Reservations

Reserving a wish tells the others it is taken, and tells the beneficiary
nothing. A reservation carries its own progress: **reserved → bought → wrapped
→ given**. What is left to do surfaces on the dashboard, so the tracker is a
to-do list rather than a status badge.

A reservation can cover several occurrences at once — the same gift for
*Christmas 2026* and a birthday, for instance — because occasions are attached
as `{ id, year }` pairs. The form offers the next occurrence and the one after
it; an occasion with no computable date, such as a name day on a profile
without one, cannot be selected.

Two people cannot reserve the same wish by accident: a partial unique index and
a row lock on the wish make the race impossible, not merely unlikely.

## Off-list gifts

Start from the action above a person's wish list, beside a household or family
member's list action, beside an upcoming occasion, or from Reservations. Recipient
and occasion prefills remain editable, including the selected occurrence year.
New gifts default to private; opening contributions is an explicit choice.

A gift that nobody asked for still needs organising. An off-list reservation is
created for a person and an occasion without any wish behind it. It can be kept
**private**, or made **visible** to the rest of the family so that others can
ask to join in — never to the beneficiary.
These choices concern preparation only. Once given, even a private gift enters
the recipient's current family history, still excluding the recipient.

## Contributions

On a reservation open to contributions, others can request to participate; the
organiser accepts or declines. Accepted participants see the gift and appear in
its history.

## History and search

Once given, a gift is kept as an independent snapshot, readable by the
recipient's current family circle, **except the recipient**, even if the gift
was private during preparation. Former organizers and participants lose history
access when they no longer share a family with the recipient. This deliberately
expands completed-history visibility, not active reservation access. It answers
the question that causes the most duplicates: *what did we already give them?*

History entries identify the gift and recipient, and show the organizer,
participants, occasion and gift date when recorded. Missing details are
omitted or labeled unavailable; when the gift date is unknown, the entry
labels its record date instead. Historical identities expose only limited
public fields, including for older snapshots.

Choose a recipient from accessible history entries to review all their recorded
occurrences. Sort newest first (default), oldest first or recipient A-Z.
Recipient order uses the latest snapshot name, then recipient ID, newest date
and history ID; date sorts use history ID for ties. Filtering never looks up
people outside authorized history. URL selections survive back/forward, which
rechecks access rather than reusing stale history. Loading, errors, no history
and no matching gifts are distinct states.

Search covers people, wishes and past gifts in shared families. Wish results
identify both the wish and the person whose list it belongs to. Hidden off-list,
deleted and gifted wishes are excluded, and reservation details stay hidden
from the wish owner. Past gifts come from the history under its exact access
rule, matched on their title and on the recorded recipient, organizer,
participant and occasion names; each result is labeled as already given and
opens the history filtered on its recipient, with the entry highlighted.
Loading and errors are explicit, and changing a query clears previous results
without moving focus out of the input.

## English and French

The whole app is bilingual. Pick a language in the interface and the preference
is stored in the browser; without one, the browser language decides. Interface
text, dates, currency and API error messages all follow the selected language —
the API picks it from `Accept-Language`.

Content entered by users is not translated, and neither is the sample data.

Translations are not a lookup table that can silently lose an entry: the
translation key type is derived from the French strings, so a missing English
counterpart fails the build.
